## The One Rule That Decides Every Run

Fart Together is a physics-based co-op game where 2–4 players steer a single shared raft using fart power, and the developers are blunt about the core lesson: the secret isn't farting harder, it's farting together at the right time. That single line explains almost everything about how online co-op works here — one mistimed push from one player can throw the whole raft off course, and one perfectly synchronized push can rescue a run at the last second.

This guide covers what the [official Fart Together Steam store page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/5117550/Fart_Together/) actually confirms about online co-op, what it leaves unconfirmed, and how to build a crew that stops blaming each other long enough to clear a course. Where the store listing is silent — matchmaking flow, invite methods, crossplay — this guide says so rather than guessing.

## How Raft Physics Actually Shapes Team Play

The raft responds to three things from each player: force, direction, and timing. That's the entire mental model you need. Because all four players are acting on one body, every input is a vote — and the raft obeys the sum of those votes, not the loudest one.

This creates a few practical consequences the store page hints at directly:

- **Speed is a team decision.** The page notes you'll sometimes need to speed up and sometimes slow down. A crew that only ever pushes forward will overshoot turns.
- **Sharp turns require asymmetry.** Handling a turn means some players act and others hold back — which requires knowing who is doing what before the turn arrives.
- **Hazards punish panic.** Hazards that throw the raft off balance mean the wrong reaction amplifies the problem instead of correcting it.
- **Arguments are part of the design.** The developers openly frame the game as one that may test friendships, with players questioning physics, directions, and each other's abilities.

If your group has someone with a questionable sense of direction or a habit of asking "What did I do?", the store page warns this game may drive you a little crazy. Plan for that socially, not just mechanically.

## Communication Tactics for a 2–4 Player Crew

Since the game rewards synchronized action, the highest-value improvement most groups can make is a shared vocabulary. These tactics are built on the confirmed mechanics — direction, timing, and gas — rather than on undocumented features.

1. **Assign a caller.** One player announces the plan ("hold, hold, now") so the crew acts on one cue instead of four overlapping ones. The caller isn't the boss; they're the metronome.
2. **Use short, unambiguous words.** "Forward," "hold," "left side," and "now" beat full sentences. In a physics game, a two-second explanation arrives after the raft has already hit the wall.
3. **Count down before coordinated pushes.** A shared "three, two, one" converts four separate inputs into one strong, straight push.
4. **Call corrections immediately and without debate.** If the raft is drifting, say the correction first and discuss blame after the run. The developers expect blame to happen — just schedule it for the results screen.
5. **Review one mistake per attempt.** Pick the single biggest error, fix that, and retry. Trying to correct four things at once usually produces four new problems.

## Handling Courses Without Losing the Run

Courses are built from a small set of confirmed obstacle types: narrow passages, sharp turns, moving obstacles, and balance-breaking hazards. Each one fails for a different reason, so each one needs a different default response.

- **Narrow passages:** prioritize straightness over speed. Drift is what clips the wall, not velocity.
- **Sharp turns:** slow down *before* the turn, not during it. Once the raft is committed, late braking usually spins the crew.
- **Moving obstacles:** watch the pattern for one cycle before entering. Reacting to an obstacle you haven't timed means reacting too late.
- **Balance hazards:** the crew should recover together. One player overcorrecting while others keep pushing is how a wobble becomes a wipe.

The developers describe the intended loop as one small mistake turning into a hilarious disaster, followed by "one more try." Expect to replay sections. That's the game working as designed.
